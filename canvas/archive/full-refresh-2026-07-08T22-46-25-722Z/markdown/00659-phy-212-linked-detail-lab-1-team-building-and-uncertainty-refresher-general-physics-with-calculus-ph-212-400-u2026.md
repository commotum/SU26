PH212 – General Physics with Calculus

Activity goal: Build community, learn how this course will work, and determine whether experimental results support a scientific claim based on uncertainty ranges.

Working with a team is one of the most crucial skills in physics and other technical disciplines. You will likely collaborate with many people throughout your academic and non-academic careers, and employers frequently cite teamwork as one of the most valuable skills in job candidates. In this lab course, all of your work will be done in groups, so practicing these skills from the start is essential. Scientific measurements never produce a single exact value. Every result includes some amount of uncertainty. In this lab, you will also revisit the ideas of measurement uncertainty and statistical uncertainty, then apply them to real experimental data. Your goal is to determine whether small differences in measured acceleration reflect a true physical effect or whether they fall within the range of normal experimental variation. By the end of the activity, you will use total uncertainty to make, and justify, a scientific conclusion.

Part 1: Experimental and Measurement Uncertainty

In real experiments, every measurement represents a range of plausible values rather than a single exact number. Uncertainty contributes to this range and describes how confident we are in a reported value.

What is Uncertainty?

Uncertainty is the acknowledgement that we cannot measure things exactly, and so any conclusion based on measurements will always include a range of possible answers. When making a measurement, there is no single “true value” of the measurand (measured quantity) that we can determine. Instead, we can define an interval that defines the range of values that we think are probable for the measurand. The narrower the interval, the more precisely we can approximate the measurand.

When including uncertainty in a value, we report it as A ± ΔA. We believe the value is A, but we’re unable to rule out the possibility that it’s higher or lower by an amount ΔA. (Sometimes it’s written +Δ1 / -Δ2 if the higher and lower ranges are different.) For example, if we’re confident a time is between 2.42 and 2.48 s, it would be reported as 2.45 s ± 0.03 s.

spacer

Measurement Uncertainty

Measurement uncertainty comes from the limits of the measuring instrument. It is based on the smallest readable increment (for visual scales) or the least-significant digit (for digital displays), and is typically reported as A ± ΔA.

For a single measurement, if you are using an instrument with a visual scale (like a ruler), the minimum uncertainty is just one half of the smallest increment you can read on the instrument.

Part 2: Applying Uncertainty to Experimental Data

Measurement uncertainty and statistical uncertainty are the recognition that every measured quantity represents a range of plausible values rather than a single exact number. In real experiments, different trials of the same measurement will never produce identical results, and small variations in the data do not automatically imply that a real physical difference exists. To make meaningful scientific conclusions, we must not only calculate measurement values but also quantify their associated uncertainties and compare whether those uncertainty ranges overlap.
