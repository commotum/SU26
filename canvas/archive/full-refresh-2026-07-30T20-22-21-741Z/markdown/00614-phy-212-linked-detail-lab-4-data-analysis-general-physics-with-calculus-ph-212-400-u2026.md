Procedure

 

  A. Setting Up the Pendulum

  1. Select a hanging mass (bob).
Choose a small, dense object to serve as the pendulum bob. Dense objects experience less air resistance and produce more reliable timing measurements.

Ideal options include a metal washer stack, a heavy key ring, or a lead fishing weight (≥ 4 oz).
Acceptable household alternatives include a can of soda, a small jar filled with coins, or a compact metal object.

The bob should be securely attached and should not deform or swing unevenly during motion.

  2. Prepare the string.

Cut a piece of string, thread, or thin cord to serve as the pendulum string.

The string should be light compared to the bob and should not stretch noticeably.
A good starting length is approximately the distance from your eye to your outstretched hand, though this will be adjusted later.

  D. Measuring the Period

  1. Release the pendulum.
Release the bob from rest without pushing it. The pendulum should begin oscillating smoothly in a single plane.

  2. Choose a timing strategy and reference point.

To reduce timing uncertainty, measure the total time required for many oscillations and divide by the number of oscillations measured to determine the period. To reduce timing uncertainty, you should time multiple oscillations rather than just one. Decide how many oscillations you will time and explain in Q1 why that number is a good choice for balancing precision and practicality. You should also choose a clear, repeatable reference point in the motion at which to start and stop the stopwatch. For example, the lowest point of the swing is often a good reference point because it is easier to identify consistently, but any well-defined point may be used if it is used consistently throughout the experiment.

  3. Count oscillations carefully.

Count each time the pendulum passes through the bottom of its swing.

Each pass corresponds to half of a cycle (e.g., if you decide to count until the pendulum completes 20 full cycles, it will correspond to 40 bottom passes).

  4. Stop the stopwatch when the pendulum reaches its desired number of passes.

  5. Calculate the period.

Use your total measured time and the number of oscillations to calculate the period of one oscillation.

What is Uncertainty?

Uncertainty is the acknowledgement that we cannot measure things exactly, and so any conclusion based on measurements will always include a range of possible answers. When making a measurement, there is no single “true value” of the measurand (measured quantity) that we can determine. Instead, we can define an interval that defines the range of values that we think are probable for the measurand. The narrower the interval, the more precisely we can approximate the measurand.

When including uncertainty in a value, we report it as A ± ΔA. We believe the value is A, but we’re unable to rule out the possibility that it’s higher or lower by an amount ΔA. (Sometimes it’s written +Δ1 / -Δ2 if the higher and lower ranges are different.) For example, if we’re confident a time is between 2.42 and 2.48 s, it would be reported as 2.45 s ± 0.03 s.

 

Common sources of uncertainty include:

your judgement in reading analogue instruments
the effects of environmental conditions on the measurement
the sensitivity of your instruments (e.g. a digital scale, a meter stick)
the rating or stated calibration of the instrument
approximations and assumptions that you make while doing the experiment
variations in repeated readings made under apparently identical conditions (statistical uncertainty)

 

.

A measurement uncertainty is not meant to be an indication of “mistakes” that you might make in an experiment. If you are aware that you have made a mistake, then you should repeat your experiment. “Human error” is not a valid source of uncertainty. If you know that you did something “wrong”, then the right course of action is to redo it correctly.

Part 4.2: Analyze the Data – Fitting

Fitting a function to a dataset is a key part of experimental work because it allows us to understand how the dependent variable relates to the independent variable. Most of the plots you’ll create will include a fit to your data. Fits are typically used to determine how well the data agrees with a particular (mathematical) model of the system’s behavior.

The correlation coefficient, R2, is one measure of how well the model fits the data. Values close to 1 signify that the fit does a good job of predicting the data, whereas values closer to zero signify that the fit poorly predicts the data. While there are no hard cutoffs, below 0.7 is indicative of a poor fit, and above 0.9 or 0.95 is generally indicative of a good or very good fit.

While you can use visual inspection to make rough evaluations of each trendline, R2 is often useful for distinguishing among equally good-looking fits. Note that the R2 value is just one of many ways to evaluate the quality of a fit.

If we have a prediction about the relationship between two quantities that is nonlinear, we can “force” a proportional relationship using a process called linearization.

In the context of data analysis, “linear” refers to a directly proportional relationship between the dependent and independent variables in an equation.

Linearization comes in handy when your data or hypothesis is not linear.

When you fit data with a linear trendline, you are essentially checking whether the two variables are proportional to each other. To check a nonlinear hypothesis, can we instead check if one variable is proportional to a function of the other variable.

In practice, linearization is done by calculating a new column of data, that gives the values of a function of the input variable. Typically, the input variable is the independent variable, and the function is chosen so that the resulting quantity is proportional to the dependent variable.

Why Linearize?

Linear data is typically easier to understand and analyze.
There are analysis methods we can apply to linear data that don’t work as well (or at all) with nonlinear data.
It is relatively easy to fit a line to data - there are just two important values to determine: the slope and intercept of the data.

What is Uncertainty?

When making a measurement, there is no single “true value” of the measurand (measured quantity) that we can determine. Sources of Uncertainty:

The uncertainty in a measurement can be due to a combination of several factors. A crucial aspect of experimentation is to identify all such sources of uncertainty and to numerically estimate their effect on your measurement result.

Common sources of uncertainty include:

the effects of environmental conditions on the measurement
your judgement in reading analogue instruments
the sensitivity of your instruments (e.g. the digital scale)
the rating or stated calibration of the instrument

Variables and Parameters

It will help you when designing your experiment to think about the different types of numerical quantities that are involved. It will make your explanation clearer if you label the different quantities appropriately.

Independent variable: In an experiment you will usually vary one quantity at a time in a controlled manner. This is the independent variable. Typically, you’re trying to discover how the independent variable affects the system.

Dependent variables: Your hypothesis should predict the behavior of other quantities in your system as the independent variable is changed. These other quantities are referred to as the dependent variables because their values depend on the value of the independent variable.

Parameters: There are other quantities you should specify because they may influence your results, but that will remain fixed throughout the experiment. These are referred to as parameters. Examples could be the length of a track or the mass of an object. The key is that a parameter doesn’t change at any time in the experiment.

In short, parameters are fixed, and variables change. The change is either under your control (independent) or a response to the experiment (dependent).
