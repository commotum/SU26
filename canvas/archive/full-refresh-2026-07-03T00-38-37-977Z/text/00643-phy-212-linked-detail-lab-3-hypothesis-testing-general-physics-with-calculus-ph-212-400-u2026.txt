Part 3: Collect Data

You are now ready to perform your experiment.

Procedure

Figure 2: Inclined plane setup for rolling objects so they enter free-fall.

Select and prepare a horizontal launch surface.
Use a level or phone app to check if the table or elevated surface is horizontal. If it's not, adjust it or find another surface.
Build your inclined plane.

Create a ramp that makes an angle between 20° and 45° with the horizontal. Secure it so that its bottom end stops a few centimeters before the edge of the table.

Smooth the transition to the table.

Tape a card or thin surface to the bottom of the ramp to help the object roll smoothly onto the horizontal surface. This ensures the object exits the table with mostly horizontal velocity.

Measure the ramp angle.
Carefully measure the angle of inclination using a protractor or app. Adjust and rebuild as needed to stay within the 20–45° range.

Figure 3: How to find the edge of the table

 

Mark the edge of the table.

Hang a string with a small object (like a paperclip) over the edge to find a vertical reference point. Let it settle, then mark the spot directly below it on the floor or on a landing sheet. This helps with accurate horizontal distance measurements.

Figure 4: When a coin lands on aluminum foil, it leaves an impression which indicates how far it travelled in free fall.

 

Prepare the landing area.
Predict where the object will land, then place a large sheet of foil, carbon copy paper, or other marking material on the floor in that area. Foil should be placed on a soft surface like a towel; carbon paper should be on a hard surface.
Conduct your first trial.

Place the object at the top of the ramp and release it without pushing. It should roll down, travel across the horizontal surface, and launch off the edge.

Mark and measure the landing point.

Record where the object hits the landing surface. Measure the horizontal distance d from the table edge to the landing point.

Repeat the trial for the same height.
Conduct multiple trials (e.g., 3 or more) for the same starting height to account for variation and improve reliability.
Repeat for additional objects.

Using the same starting height, repeat steps 7–9 for each of the remaining objects. Ensure that all objects are released from the same position on the ramp so that they begin with the same drop height. Record the horizontal range for each object to compare how the launch distance depends on rotational inertia.

What is Uncertainty?

Uncertainty is the acknowledgement that we cannot measure things exactly, and so any conclusion based on measurements will always include a range of possible answers. When making a measurement, there is no single “true value” of the measurand (measured quantity) that we can determine. Instead, we can define an interval that defines the range of values that we think are probable for the measurand. The narrower the interval, the more precisely we can approximate the measurand.

When including uncertainty in a value, we report it as A ± ΔA. We believe the value is A, but we’re unable to rule out the possibility that it’s higher or lower by an amount ΔA. (Sometimes it’s written +Δ1 / -Δ2 if the higher and lower ranges are different.) For example, a time might be reported as 2.45 s ± 0.03 s.

 

Sources of Uncertainty:

The uncertainty in a measurement can be due to a combination of several factors. A crucial aspect of experimentation is to identify all such sources of uncertainty and to numerically estimate their effect on your measurement result.

Common sources of uncertainty include:

the effects of environmental conditions on the measurement
your judgement in reading analogue instruments
the sensitivity of your instruments (e.g. the digital scale)
the rating or stated calibration of the instrument
approximations and assumptions that you make while doing the experiment
variations in repeated readings made under apparently identical conditions

 

Variables and Parameters

It will help you when designing your experiment to think about the different types of numerical quantities that are involved. It will make your explanation clearer if you label the different quantities appropriately.

Independent variable: In an experiment you will usually vary one quantity at a time in a controlled manner. This is the independent variable. Typically, you’re trying to discover how the independent variable affects the system.

Dependent variables: Your hypothesis should predict the behavior of other quantities in your system as the independent variable is changed. These other quantities are referred to as the dependent variables because their values depend on the value of the independent variable.

Parameters: There are other quantities you should specify because they may influence your results, but that will remain fixed throughout the experiment. These are referred to as parameters. Examples could be the length of a track or the mass of an object. The key is that a parameter doesn’t change at any time in the experiment.

In short, parameters are fixed, and variables change. The change is either under your control (independent) or a response to the experiment (dependent).
